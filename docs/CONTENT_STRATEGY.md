# Nextdecode short-form content system

## Audience and promise

**Audience:** English-speaking, tech-curious viewers who want to understand useful AI and technology without hype.

**Promise:** one surprising but supportable idea, explained clearly, with a practical implication.

The system should optimize for viewers choosing to watch, staying through the payoff, and finding the video satisfying. No posting formula can promise virality or a specific number of views. YouTube says Shorts recommendations are personalized and use signals such as chose-to-view, average view duration, average percentage viewed, and viewer satisfaction; topic interest and competition also change distribution. [YouTube Shorts discovery guidance](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/11914225)

## Repeatable series

Choose the series that fits the evidence. Rotate across runs when the topic supports it; never force a weak topic just to fill a slot.

1. **AI You Can Use** — one concrete tool or workflow, who it helps, and one real limitation. Demonstrate steps only when the source or a reproducible check supports them.
2. **Hype Check** — take a specific claim and compare it with what the paper, product documentation, or credible report actually establishes. Separate evidence from speculation.
3. **What Changed** — explain a genuinely recent release, research result, or industry shift; say what changed and who should care.
4. **How It Works** — explain one hard concept with a simple analogy, then state where the analogy stops working.
5. **Startup Breakdown** — explain the user problem, product mechanism, business model, and one trade-off using sourced facts.

Avoid generic “AI is changing everything” stories, unsupported rankings, invented statistics, recycled motivational/psychology content, exaggerated earnings claims, and summaries that add no original explanation.

## Story structure

- First line: a specific, truthful curiosity hook. No greeting or fake suspense.
- Middle: one idea, with the source-backed fact or mechanism early.
- Payoff: a useful action, consequence, or caveat that resolves the hook.
- Keep narration roughly 70–90 words for the current 30–40 second render. Use plain spoken English and explain unfamiliar terms.
- Use sources discovered for this run. Preserve links in the platform descriptions. Do not turn a headline into a fact without checking the linked source.
- Keep the master video original: Nextdecode narration, captions, and visual explanation. Avoid third-party reposts and watermarks.

## Long-form editorial standard

Long-form should be a focused mini-documentary, not a list of unrelated headlines. Before writing, compare multiple current candidates for viewer relevance, observed interest signals, evidence quality, originality, and visual potential. These are editorial judgments, not measured demand or a guarantee of reach.

Use the primary source as the spine of the story and seek independent reporting on the same result. Read both source pages where accessible; capture exact supporting passages, separate what each source establishes from what it does not, and state the remaining evidence gap. Do not treat a feed excerpt or abstract as a full research review. If the primary or corroborating source cannot be retrieved, stop and request a different topic or manual research instead of fabricating detail.

Open on a concrete, truthful tension—never a greeting. Establish the viewer promise quickly, explain one mechanism with a tangible analogy or demonstration, show evidence before broad implications, and close with a useful payoff. Every chapter should earn its place and have an intentional visual treatment. Do not use a generic diagram for every topic; use the relevant licensed footage, owner-recorded demonstration, or a topic-specific original explainer. If the only available visuals are procedural placeholders, the video stays draft-only.

After publication, use actual YouTube analytics (impressions click-through rate, first-30-second retention, average view duration, and audience retention dips) to revise the next topic, title, opening, and pacing. Until those channel analytics are available, topic choices are reasoned editorial estimates—not proven audience winners.

## Platform packaging

The same clean 9:16 master can be distributed across platforms, but metadata is written for each audience context:

- **YouTube Shorts:** short searchable title; concise description; verified source links; stable topic/category tags. Review “shown in feed,” “chose to view,” retention, and subscribers gained in YouTube Studio.
- **Instagram Reels:** conversational caption with a useful save/share reason and a few topic-relevant hashtags. Keep the first on-screen spoken line compelling; add any licensed/native trending audio in Instagram itself when appropriate.
- **Facebook Reels:** plain-language title and a little context in the description, with a genuine topic question. Publish original work; Meta says copied or lightly altered content may be deprioritized. [Meta original-content guidance](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/03/rewarding-original-creators-on-facebook/)

Instagram’s in-app Best Practices area can give account-specific recommendations; revisit it as the account develops. [Instagram creator Best Practices](https://about.fb.com/news/2024/10/best-practices-education-hub-creators-instagram/)

## Improvement loop

Review each platform separately because its audience and metrics differ. After each batch, compare series on opening retention, average watch time/completion, saves/shares, comments, and follows/subscribers. Keep the strongest *topic + hook + payoff* combinations, revise weak openings, and stop repeating formats that underperform. Do not infer success from one viral outlier or view count alone.

This WSL workflow currently publishes when all destination setup is complete. It does not yet fetch platform analytics automatically, so performance-based learning still requires checking each platform’s creator dashboard and using those observations to guide the next topic batch.
